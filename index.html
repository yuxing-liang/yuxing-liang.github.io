Job Market Paper

Why Students Can Misreport under Strategy-Proof Matching

Draft available upon request

Request Draft Presentation Slides: SSCW 2026, Tokyo

Abstract

Strategy-proofness makes truthful reporting optimal, but it need not make truth uniquely dominant. This paper characterizes the preference comparisons that a student's assignment incentives identify when a matching mechanism requires a complete ranking. For any deterministic strategy-proof mechanism, a report is dominant exactly when it chooses the same school as truth from every menu that rival reports can generate. Dominance therefore identifies precisely the comparisons that some attainable menu makes jointly decisive.

Serial Dictatorship provides the benchmark. Under Deferred Acceptance (DA) and Top Trading Cycles (TTC), reports truthful through a student-specific guarantee rank are always dominant, and they are exactly the dominant reports for every priority profile if and only if total capacity exceeds enrollment. With one spare seat, DA, TTC, and a broader class of strategy-proof mechanisms identify the same comparisons. Without a spare seat, dominant reports need not form a truthful prefix. The report domain is decisive: allowing rivals to truncate removes this capacity boundary.

In the empirical application, I reanalyze publicly available student-report data from the Chen–Sönmez DA study. Of 58 reports that differ literally from the induced true ranking, 38 preserve the truthful assignment at the realized profile, while only six are dominant. This classification isolates the assignment-incentive component of nontruthful reporting from coincidental harmlessness and assignment-relevant mistakes.

Working Paper · Submitted

Towards a Better Major Assignment: An Empirical Study with Students in China

with Xintong Han and Yan Zeng

This paper studies centralized major assignment in Chinese higher education using students' mock and official rank-order lists, entrance-exam records, and information about their application environment. We document how students revise their choices as they learn about competition for majors and estimate their preferences using discrete-choice methods.

We then use the estimated preferences to compare alternative assignment mechanisms through counterfactual analysis. The comparison evaluates welfare, first-choice placement, distributional outcomes, and the treatment of lower-ranked and female students. By connecting observed preference formation to mechanism performance, the paper provides an empirical market-design analysis of college major assignment.

Work in Progress

Progressive Choice Mechanisms in School Choice Problems

with Szilvia Pápai

This paper studies a family of school-choice mechanisms that varies how many options each student may access at each step. Under common priorities, the family connects Immediate Acceptance, intermediate Chinese parallel mechanisms, and Serial Dictatorship; under school-specific priorities, its fully permissive endpoint is student-proposing Deferred Acceptance. We characterize efficiency and how expanding choice opportunities changes manipulability, and we study the corresponding stability trade-offs.

Complementary Strengths

Computational Methods for Matching Research

Computation is integrated into my matching research. I implement assignment mechanisms, enumerate finite markets, search for counterexamples, verify conjectures, and run simulations that clarify how theoretical boundaries change across market environments.

Empirical analysis complements the theory. I construct reproducible preference and assignment datasets, estimate discrete-choice models, and connect observed rank-order lists to mechanism-based counterfactuals in college and major assignment.
